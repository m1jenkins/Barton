# Formats

Skeletons for each kind of piece, taken from how Edmunds lays them out. Lengths are what Edmunds runs; go shorter when the page calls for it (Steer Me Right vehicle pages keep prose to a sentence or two per section). Headings here describe the slot; write your own wording.

## Model review page

The page a shopper lands on for one model. Top to bottom:

1. **Verdict blurb**, 2-3 sentences beside the score: what it is, the range of jobs it does, the standout version. Written so it works alone.
2. **Pros** (3-4) and **cons** (2-3). See [Pros and cons](#pros-and-cons).
3. **What's new**, 2-3 bullets: this year's changes, then which generation it belongs to and when that began. "Minor changes to feature availability" is an acceptable bullet; don't inflate a carryover year.
4. **Overview**, one paragraph, 4-6 sentences: why this car matters in its class, where it sits in the maker's lineup, the two or three qualities that explain its appeal, closing on a plain summing-up. Byline and date.
5. **Rated categories**, each a question heading, a score if we have one of our own, and 1-4 short paragraphs. First sentence answers the question. Say which trim and powertrain was tested. Order and contents are in SKILL.md section 4.
6. **Buying tips for this month.** See [Monthly buying tips](#monthly-buying-tips).
7. **Trim differences**, one paragraph: how many trims, named in order; body styles; powertrains; drivetrain. Link to the full trim guide.
8. **The trim we'd buy**, 3-4 sentences: the pick, why it beats the one below, one or two options worth paying for and who they suit.
9. **Rivals to consider**, one paragraph: 3-5 named rivals, each with its single defining strength in a clause. It can end by pointing to the maker's own alternatives a size up or down.
10. **Spotlight**, one paragraph on the single most interesting thing about this car this year (the redesign, the breadth of the lineup, a new powertrain).
11. **Living with it**, if there is long-term ownership experience: how many miles, what model year, and that it's the same generation so the notes still apply.
12. Reference blocks: specs, reliability, recalls, warranty, safety ratings, cost to drive. These are data with one framing sentence each, not prose.
13. **FAQ.** See [FAQ answers](#faq-answers).

## Ranked comparison test

Two to four cars, driven back to back, ranked.

1. **Headline**: the cars, then the question or the result ("Can the redesigned X dethrone Y?", "The X can finally keep up").
2. **Deck**, one line: why this test, now.
3. **Three-line box** at the top:
   - *What's new*: the car that prompted the test, its price, the headline change.
   - *Why it matters*: the stakes in the segment.
   - *Our take*: the result in two sentences. Give the winner away here. (Edmunds labels this line with its own name; use "Our take" or "Steer Me Right's take".)
4. **Intro**, two paragraphs: where the challenger stood before and what changed; then how the test was matched and why (which trims, which engines, why not the car already in the fleet). A one-line teaser of the result is fine.
5. **One section per car, last place first.** Each opens with:
   - Place and name as the heading
   - Score, if there is one, with its source
   - *The good*: 2-3 short phrases
   - *The bad*: 2-3 short phrases

   Then 3-5 paragraphs: why it landed here, what it does best with the proof, where it loses to the others by name, its as-tested price and what that buys. The loser's section says who should still buy it. The winner's section still lists real complaints, in detail.
6. **Close**, one paragraph: all of them are good (if true, with the evidence), the winner and the one-clause reason, then the buyer who should pick the runner-up, and a nudge to test-drive.

Keep the cars' prices and trims level, or say plainly where they aren't. Compare on the same axis in each section so the reader can line them up: if you give one car's cargo figure, give them all.

## Two-car head-to-head

The short version for a comparison page or a buyer deciding between two.

1. **Verdict paragraph**, 3-4 sentences: what each is known for, then which is the better overall car and the two reasons. Don't split the difference.
2. **Where each wins**, as two short lists or 3-5 paired paragraphs by category (price, driving, room, tech, efficiency, warranty, ownership cost). One number each, same trim level, same units.
3. **Side-by-side table** for the specs: price, power, mpg, cargo, rear legroom, warranty. Numbers live here so the prose doesn't have to recite them.
4. **Pick this one if / pick that one if**, two lines each.

## First drive or tested piece

A bylined piece, first person, about one new or updated vehicle.

1. Headline with a point of view after the colon ("Great EV, Bad Luxury SUV", "Smoothing the Edges").
2. Deck, then the three-line box (what's new, why it matters, our take with price or expected price).
3. Dateline if it was a launch event ("— Tucson, Arizona").
4. **Opening paragraph** states the thesis, often as a tension: it gets these things right and these wrong. First drives of mild updates say so.
5. **2-4 sections with plain or lightly playful headings**: what changed (hardware, figures, with the rival's figures beside them), what it's like from the seat, the cabin, the price.
6. **Test results table** when there is instrumented data: battery or engine, power, weight, 0-60, quarter mile, braking, skidpad, sound levels, price as tested. Prose then interprets two or three of those rows against the EPA figure or a rival, with the conditions noted (temperature, route).
7. **Verdict section**: who should buy it, who should wait or look elsewhere, and whether an owner of the outgoing model should trade. Predictions are labelled: "expect", "pricing hasn't been announced".

"I" is allowed, as are a personal aside and one joke. Maker claims not yet verified are attributed ("Chevrolet says", "BMW quotes").

## Alternatives roundup

"Shopping for X? Consider these."

1. Three-line box: what's new with X, why shoppers should look around, the 2-3 alternatives named.
2. **Two paragraphs on X itself**: engines, power, prices including destination, and the one trait that might put a buyer off (the reason to look elsewhere).
3. **Each alternative**: price range, score with source, then 2-3 paragraphs: its strongest case against X in numbers, its cabin and tech in contrast to X, its downsides stated flat ("rear seat room is tight, and the trunk holds only 10.5 cubic feet").

No ranking and no closing paragraph needed; each alternative is framed by which buyer it suits.

## Trim guide

1. **The pick**, first, 3 sentences: which trim, why, and the specific extras it adds over the base.
2. **Lineup paragraph**: number of trims named in order, powertrain, drivetrain choices, output.
3. **Each trim**, a heading and a lead-in line, then a bullet list of noun phrases:
   - Base: "Starts the lineup with:"
   - Next: "Has the [lower trim]'s features, plus:"
   - A trim that drops things: say so ("loses the sport styling but keeps...")
   - A themed trim: one clause on the theme before the list ("built around an outdoorsy look")
4. **Standard driver aids** once, under the base trim, each with its parenthetical plain-English gloss.
5. Table of trims side by side: starting MSRP, typical price paid where sourced, then feature rows.

Bullets are features only: no adjectives, no sentences, sizes included ("12.9-inch touchscreen", "18-inch black wheels").

## Rankings blurb

For a list of the best cars in a class.

- **Class intro**, 2 sentences: who the class is for, and its built-in tradeoff ("the third row will be cramped for anyone larger than a child, but it's nice to have the option").
- **Per car**, 2-3 sentences: the reason it ranks, two concrete strengths, and the one reservation or the buyer it won't suit. Then the data line: price range, combined mpg, year last redesigned.

## Monthly buying tips

Dated to the month, numbers first.

1. **Lead with the market headline** in one sentence: how fast the car is selling against the average, and how much supply is out there. That tells the buyer how much leverage they have.
2. **Discount**: how far under MSRP buyers are paying, in dollars or percent, and which trims have the most room.
3. **Cash offers and who qualifies.**
4. **Financing**: the best promotional rate and term, and whether longer terms are actually worse.
5. **Lease**: monthly payment, due at signing, term and mileage; the cheapest trim to lease.

Each bullet ends on what to do with the number. On Steer Me Right pages these are estimates with a date and a source, never a promise.

## Pros and cons

- 3-4 pros, 2-3 cons. If you can only find one con, you haven't looked hard enough; if you have five, keep the three a buyer would feel.
- Sentence fragments, 4-10 words, no full stop, no "and" lists of three.
- Concrete and checkable: "High maximum towing and payload capacities", "Seats lack comfort for long drives". Not "great performance" or "some drawbacks".
- A con can carry its condition: "Hybrid is noisy when powering the onboard generator".
- Order by how much a buyer will care, not by category.

## FAQ answers

Questions are the ones shoppers type: Is it a good car? Is it reliable? What's new this year? How much should I pay? What are the different versions?

Answer in the first sentence, with the number: the score and what it means, the rating and its source, the price range. Then 2-3 sentences of support and where to look next. Each answer stands alone, since it may be read without the rest of the page.
